# TEEHC™

## The Ecological Equivalent of Hyperscale Computing

![alt text](<TEEHC node map with Logo.jpg>)

## The Problem

Artificial intelligence infrastructure is expanding rapidly. The facilities required to power modern AI are becoming critical to healthcare, scientific discovery, education, industry, and communication.

Most of this infrastructure is being built the same way: massive single-site facilities optimized for compute density and cost efficiency. Everything else, including water consumption, grid strain, community impact, and ecological disruption, is treated as an externality to be managed after the fact.

The results are visible. Aquifer drawdowns in Arizona. Noise complaints from residential neighbors in Virginia. Grid instability in Texas. Communities that host these facilities often bear the costs without sharing proportionally in the benefits.

Canada is behind on AI infrastructure deployment. That lag is usually framed as a disadvantage.

It is actually an opportunity.

We have a window to build differently, before the default hyperscale model becomes the standard and the same patterns repeat.

---

# The Question

TEEHC began with a simple question:

> Can we build the next generation of compute infrastructure in a way that leaves the surrounding ecosystem stronger than before?

Not just less harmful. Stronger.

Can computing infrastructure become more deeply integrated with the environments that support it? Can energy be used more efficiently? Can excess heat become a useful resource? Can water be managed more thoughtfully? Can infrastructure contribute to resilience, restoration, and long-term value creation?

These questions led to TEEHC.

---

# The Concept

TEEHC proposes a distributed approach to AI infrastructure: a network of smaller, regionally adapted compute nodes that collectively provide hyperscale-scale computing as a unified platform, rather than concentrating capacity within a single location.

Instead of one 200 MW campus in a single location, build a federation of smaller facilities. Each one designed for its regional context. Each one integrated into its local community. All of them operating as unified infrastructure.

**One network. Many nodes.**

A node in northern Ontario uses ambient winter air for cooling, eliminating evaporative water loss for most of the year. A Prairie node taps geothermal resources. Atlantic Canada has access to cold ocean water. The Yukon offers some of the coldest ambient temperatures on the continent.

Each facility is scaled to its community. Significant enough to contribute meaningfully to the local economy. Small enough to function as a neighbor rather than an enclave.

Together, the nodes form a resilient computing network. Workloads route across the network based on capacity, efficiency, carbon intensity, or latency requirements. Redundancy is geographic, not concentrated. No single point of failure.

From an operational standpoint, customers interact with TEEHC as a single entity. From a community standpoint, each node is accountable to the place that hosts it.

TEEHC is intended as an open framework rather than a closed ownership model. Nodes may be owned and operated by municipalities, utilities, universities, Indigenous organizations, private companies, or other qualified partners. Some facilities may participate as part of a federated TEEHC network, while others may choose to adopt stewardship principles and the Stewardship Performance Index (SPI) independently.

# Design Principles

## Ecology

Computing infrastructure can work alongside natural systems rather than displacing them. Biodiversity, habitat restoration, native planting, and stormwater management become part of infrastructure design, not afterthoughts.

## Water

Water can be captured, recycled, and managed as a precious resource. Non-potable sources, closed-loop cooling, and aggressive reuse targets reduce municipal water demand. Public reporting makes water performance visible.

## Energy

Efficiency is designed in, not optimized later. Peak demand is managed to reduce grid strain. Renewable integration is prioritized. Recovered heat is directed to productive use through greenhouses, district heating, aquaculture, industrial processes, and other beneficial applications.

## Communities

Infrastructure contributes to the places where it operates. Local hiring, skilled trades, procurement, education, research partnerships, and workforce development create value that remains in the region. Facilities become long-term community partners rather than simply occupying land.

## Long-Term Resilience

Infrastructure designed for future generations, not just present needs. Adaptable. Modular. Built for changing conditions. Systems that remain valuable and relevant over decades.

# Accountability: The Stewardship Performance Index (SPI)

Concepts are easy to claim. Performance is harder to verify.

![alt text](<SPI Graphic2.jpg>)

TEEHC proposes the **Stewardship Performance Index (SPI)** as a public accountability framework for every node in the network.

SPI measures stewardship, not compute.

It asks one simple question:

> **Is this facility leaving its host community better than it found it?**

## Five Pillars (20 points each)

| Pillar       | What It Measures                                                                        |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Water        | Recycling rates, non-potable use, reduction in municipal demand, public reporting       |
| Energy       | Efficiency, peak demand management, renewable integration, grid impact mitigation       |
| Ecology      | Habitat restoration, biodiversity enhancement, native planting, stormwater improvements |
| Community    | Local hiring, skills training, partnerships, educational opportunities                  |
| Transparency | Public reporting, independent audits, open metrics, community engagement                |

## SPI Scale

|    Score | Rating                 |
| -------: | ---------------------- |
|   90–100 | Exemplary Stewardship  |
|    75–89 | Strong Stewardship     |
|    60–74 | Acceptable Stewardship |
| Below 60 | Improvement Required   |

Every TEEHC node publishes its SPI score.

The network publishes an aggregate score.

Communities can track their local facility's performance.

Investors, policymakers, and researchers can compare nodes and evaluate the network as a whole.

This is how stewardship moves beyond aspiration and becomes something measurable.

# Why This Architecture

The distributed model is not just environmentally preferable. It is structurally resilient.

**Climate optimization.** Each node uses cooling strategies suited to its geography. Canada's climate is an asset, but only if facilities are designed to use it.

**Grid resilience.** Smaller facilities integrate with local grids rather than overwhelming them. No single utility negotiation can block the entire network.

**Community scale.** A 40 MW facility can become a genuine economic participant through skilled employment, local procurement, maintenance, education, research partnerships, and productive heat reuse opportunities.

**Regulatory diversification.** Provincial variation becomes a feature. If one jurisdiction tightens water rules, other nodes compensate. The network adapts.

**Graceful failure.** Redundancy across geography is more resilient than redundancy within a single campus.

The tradeoff is coordination cost. Distributed infrastructure is harder to build and operate than a single concentrated facility. That calculus changes when the costs of water litigation, community opposition, stranded assets from regulatory change, and reputational damage are considered alongside traditional construction costs.

---

# What's Needed

TEEHC is a concept. Making it real requires:

* Policy frameworks that recognize and reward distributed, stewardship-first infrastructure.
* Provincial permitting that evaluates proposals against SPI-like criteria.
* Federal incentives tied to measurable accountability.
* A pilot consisting of two connected nodes, one in Ontario and one in a northern or Atlantic location, demonstrating that the federated model works technically, economically, and as a community partnership.
* Partners willing to build differently, including municipalities, developers, investors, utilities, researchers, and educational institutions.
* Visibility. The opportunity to establish a stewardship-first model exists today. Once conventional hyperscale infrastructure becomes entrenched, changing course becomes significantly more difficult.

# Looking Forward

TEEHC is an ongoing exploration of what becomes possible when stewardship is considered alongside performance, reliability, efficiency, and scale.

The goal is simple:

**Infrastructure that future generations will not have to apologize for.**

The questions are worth exploring.

The window is worth using.

The future of AI infrastructure will be measured not only by the intelligence it enables, but by the legacy it leaves behind.

TEEHC explores whether compute and stewardship can advance together, creating infrastructure that communities are proud to host rather than willing to tolerate.
