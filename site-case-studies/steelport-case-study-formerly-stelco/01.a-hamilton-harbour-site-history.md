# Hamilton Harbour: Industrialization, Degradation, and Recovery

## Overview

Hamilton Harbour is one of the most significant environmental recovery stories in Canada.

Today, the harbour sits at the centre of a complex relationship between industry, ecology, transportation, and community. It has supported economic growth for generations while also carrying the environmental consequences of that growth.

The history of Hamilton Harbour is not a simple story of decline or recovery.

It is a story of transformation.

Understanding that history provides important context for any future redevelopment along the harbour shoreline, including the Steelport project.

---

## Before Industrialization

Long before Hamilton became an industrial city, the harbour was a productive natural ecosystem.

The harbour is a sheltered bay connected to Lake Ontario and protected by the Burlington Beach sandbar. Its calm waters, wetlands, and natural shoreline habitats supported fish, birds, wildlife, and Indigenous communities for thousands of years.

The harbour was known to Indigenous peoples as *Macassa*, often translated as "beautiful waters."

Extensive coastal wetlands surrounded the bay. These wetlands filtered water, provided habitat, moderated flooding, and supported a rich diversity of plant and animal life.

The harbour's geography also made it one of the most naturally protected harbours on western Lake Ontario.

Those same characteristics would later make it attractive for shipping and industry.

---

## Industrial Growth

Industrial development accelerated during the nineteenth century and continued throughout much of the twentieth century.

The harbour became a centre for:

* Steel production
* Coal handling
* Coke production
* Shipping
* Manufacturing
* Petroleum-related activities
* Rail transportation
* Port infrastructure

Large portions of the natural shoreline were altered or filled to create industrial land.

Wetlands were reduced.

Natural shoreline habitat was replaced with docks, rail yards, industrial facilities, shipping terminals, and land reclamation projects.

As Hamilton grew, municipal wastewater systems and urban runoff also discharged into the harbour.

At the time, environmental regulations were limited and pollution controls were often absent.

Industrial development brought employment, economic growth, and national importance to the region.

It also placed increasing pressure on the harbour ecosystem.

---

## Environmental Degradation

By the mid-twentieth century, environmental conditions within Hamilton Harbour had deteriorated significantly.

The harbour received contaminants from multiple sources over many decades.

These included:

* Industrial discharges
* Municipal wastewater
* Urban stormwater runoff
* Contaminated sediments
* Airborne deposition from industrial emissions

Contaminants identified within harbour sediments included:

* Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)
* Heavy metals
* Petroleum-related compounds
* Other industrial pollutants

The cumulative effect of these inputs was substantial.

Fish habitat declined.

Water quality deteriorated.

Wildlife populations were affected.

Large areas of the harbour became ecologically impaired.

The most severe contamination occurred within harbour sediments, where pollutants accumulated over generations.

Unlike many forms of pollution, contaminated sediment can continue affecting an ecosystem long after the original source has been reduced or eliminated.

---

## Area of Concern Designation

In 1987, Hamilton Harbour was designated as one of the Great Lakes Areas of Concern by the International Joint Commission.

Areas of Concern represent locations where environmental degradation has impaired the health of the ecosystem.

Hamilton Harbour became one of only 43 such locations identified across the Great Lakes basin.

The designation reflected a broad range of environmental issues, including:

* Contaminated sediments
* Loss of fish and wildlife habitat
* Water quality degradation
* Restrictions on fish consumption
* Impacts on aquatic ecosystems
* Beach closures
* Degraded aesthetics
* Limitations on recreational use

The designation marked an important turning point.

For the first time, restoration of the harbour became a coordinated long-term objective shared by multiple levels of government, industry, conservation organizations, researchers, and community groups.

---

## The Hamilton Harbour Remedial Action Plan

The Hamilton Harbour Remedial Action Plan (HHRAP) was established in the early 1990s as a long-term strategy for restoring the harbour.

The plan focused on identifying environmental impairments and developing actions to address them.

Key priorities included:

* Improving wastewater treatment
* Reducing industrial pollution
* Restoring fish habitat
* Restoring wildlife habitat
* Improving water quality
* Managing contaminated sediments
* Increasing public awareness and engagement

Unlike many infrastructure projects, the Remedial Action Plan was measured in decades rather than years.

Recovery of a heavily industrialized harbour requires long-term commitment and continuous monitoring.

---

## Measurable Progress

Over the past several decades, substantial progress has been made.

Numerous wastewater treatment upgrades have reduced pollutant inputs.

Industrial emissions have declined significantly compared with historical levels.

Habitat restoration projects have reintroduced wetland and shoreline features.

Water quality has improved.

Fish and wildlife populations have shown signs of recovery.

Several indicators frequently cited by restoration organizations include:

* Return of bald eagles to the harbour area after decades of absence
* Recovery of native aquatic vegetation in portions of the harbour
* Improved fish habitat
* Improved water clarity in some areas
* Expanded recreational opportunities

Many environmental indicators that once showed severe impairment have improved or are approaching recovery targets.

The harbour remains under active management, but conditions today are markedly different from those of the mid-twentieth century.

---

## Randle Reef

The most significant remaining contamination challenge within Hamilton Harbour is Randle Reef.

Located in the southwest corner of the harbour, Randle Reef became the largest contaminated sediment site on the Canadian side of the Great Lakes.

Approximately 615,000 cubic metres of contaminated sediment accumulated across roughly 60 hectares.

The contamination consists primarily of PAHs associated with historical industrial activity.

For decades, Randle Reef was considered one of the largest barriers to full harbour restoration.

The cleanup project began in 2015.

The remediation strategy involves:

### Stage 1

Construction of an engineered containment facility.

### Stage 2

Dredging contaminated sediments and placing them within the containment facility.

### Stage 3

Final capping, habitat restoration, and completion of the project.

As of 2026, the project is approaching its final stages, with completion anticipated in 2027.

The completion of Randle Reef remediation is expected to remove one of the most significant remaining environmental obstacles within Hamilton Harbour.

---

## Recovery Still Underway

Although major progress has been achieved, harbour recovery remains an ongoing process.

Several areas continue to receive attention, including:

* Habitat restoration
* Shoreline rehabilitation
* Stormwater management
* Water quality monitoring
* Fish and wildlife monitoring
* Public access improvements
* Long-term sediment management

Recovery is not a single project.

It is a continuing process that requires ongoing investment, monitoring, and stewardship.

The harbour's future condition will depend on decisions made over many decades.

---

## Why This Matters for Steelport

The Steelport redevelopment is taking place during a period of significant transition for Hamilton Harbour.

The harbour is no longer defined solely by its industrial past.

Nor has recovery been fully completed.

Both realities exist simultaneously.

The site sits within a landscape that has experienced more than a century of industrial development and more than three decades of coordinated restoration efforts.

Future development will become part of the harbour's next chapter.

How that chapter is written remains an open question.
